THE BIG PICTURE

More greenhouse gases retain more heat in the Earth system.

A warmer world changes heat, rainfall, oceans and ice. Start with the measurements, compare possible futures, or explore your country. Future outcomes depend on emissions as well as local conditions.

WHY SOME CLIMATE NUMBERS LOOK DIFFERENT — BASELINES, UNITS AND DATA PRODUCTS
TEMPERATURE BASELINES

The NASA headline above compares a year with 1951–1980. WMO reports 2025 as 1.43 ± 0.13°C above 1850–1900. Both can be correct because the reference periods differ.

CO₂ PRODUCTS

The latest complete-year NOAA value above is the annual mean at Mauna Loa. WMO's latest consolidated global mean is 423.9 ppm for 2024. Location, averaging and release date matter.

UNITS

ppm means molecules per million air molecules; Gt is a billion tonnes; million km² is a million square kilometres. They describe different parts of the same Earth system.

Terms, units and how updates work

Anomaly / baseline: a change compared with a named reference period. +1°C is a difference, not the actual temperature.

SSP: Shared Socioeconomic Pathway — a possible emissions future, not a forecast. The final number, such as 4.5, denotes the approximate added warming influence in watts per square metre by 2100; it is not degrees Celsius.

CMIP6: the sixth international climate-model comparison project. An ensemble means results from several models. ERA5: past weather estimated by combining observations with a physical model.

Median / P10–P90: the middle result and the 10th–90th percentile range across model results. The range describes model spread, not guaranteed limits.

ppm: parts per million, used for CO₂ concentration. Gt: a billion tonnes. m w.e.: metres of water equivalent — melted ice expressed as water thickness. J: joules, a unit of heat energy. pH: acidity; a lower pH means more acidic water.

IPCC: Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change, which assesses climate research. WMO: World Meteorological Organization. NASA / NOAA: US space and ocean–atmosphere agencies. NSIDC: National Snow and Ice Data Center.

Updates: global series are checked daily. A retrieval date is not a measurement date; annual indicators use completed years and sea ice uses completed Septembers. Country profiles renew on the next visit after seven days. Published assessment figures change only after editorial review.

Scientific ranges: IPCC “likely” means 66–100% assessed probability; “very likely” means 90–100%. These describe the stated outcome under a scenario, not the probability of the scenario itself.

Days when the daily maximum reaches at least 35°C, averaged across each country. These are climate conditions, not the number of people exposed or a health-risk score. Humidity, warm nights and local conditions also matter.

Three possible futures — and what separates them

The lowest global pathway here is SSP1–1.9; the country data start at SSP1–2.6, a different, less stringent low-emissions pathway. These IPCC pathways answer “what follows if emissions develop this way?” They are not odds, promises or a continuation of a single trend line. Temperature is shown relative to 1850–1900; sea level relative to 1995–2014.

WHY SEA-LEVEL PATHWAYS ARE CLOSER IN 2050

By 2050, assessed global mean sea-level rise spans roughly +0.15 to +0.29 m across the low to very-high pathways. The larger separation comes later: cumulative emissions determine how far warming and long-lived sea-level rise proceed.

FROM EMISSIONS TO CONSEQUENCES

What happens — and why it matters

This is a causal reading guide, not a claim that every location changes in the same way.

01 · DRIVER

Greenhouse gases rise

CO₂, methane and nitrous oxide reduce the amount of heat escaping to space.

02 · ENERGY

Earth gains heat

Most of the excess heat enters the ocean; air and land also warm.

03 · PHYSICAL CHANGE

Water and ice respond

Ice melts, sea level rises, evaporation increases and rainfall patterns shift.

04 · CONSEQUENCES

Exposure becomes impact

Effects depend on where people and ecosystems are exposed and how well they can adapt.

Heat

Hot extremes become more frequent and intense, increasing health, labour and cooling risks.

Heavy rainfall & flooding

A warmer atmosphere can hold more moisture, intensifying heavy precipitation in many regions.

Drought & fire weather

Some regions face stronger drying, water stress and conditions more favourable to fire.

Coasts

Higher mean sea level raises the baseline for storm surge, erosion, salinisation and flooding.

Ocean & ecosystems

Ocean warming, acidification and deoxygenation increase risks to coral reefs and marine food webs.

People & systems

Food, water, health and infrastructure risks interact; vulnerability and adaptation shape the final outcome.

OCEAN HEAT—

The ocean absorbs most excess heat in the Earth system. The displayed NOAA record integrates the upper 2,000 metres.

GLACIERS−1.3 m w.e.

Reference glaciers lost about 1.3 metres of water-equivalent thickness in 2023/24. Estimated worldwide glacier mass loss was about 450 billion tonnes (Gt). Slovenia and Venezuela have lost all modern glaciers.

ARCTICBefore 2050

At least one effectively ice-free September is likely before 2050 across the assessed scenarios. This means less than one million km² of sea ice, not zero ice or an ice-free Arctic every year.

OCEAN ACIDIFICATIONpH declining

Human-produced CO₂ absorbed by the ocean changes carbonate chemistry, with regional differences and ecosystem consequences.

COASTAL EXTREMESMore frequent

Rising mean sea level raises the baseline for storm surge, erosion, saltwater intrusion and coastal flooding.

BEYOND 2100Deep uncertainty

Sea level continues rising for centuries. Long-horizon ice-sheet outcomes must be shown as assessed ranges, not precise forecasts.
